# How: "10 Great Films to Watch for Cinematography" (wolfcrow, 8:36, 2020)

**Type:** educational video-essay countdown (not an ad). Useful for the cinematography principles it names and its edit format: clips under VO with lower-third cards.

## Structure (timestamps)
- **0:00 cold open:** "Hi I'm Stanley Kubrick", then a self-correction joke.
- **0:05–0:51 rules:** the selection criteria. The whole film must be strong, not one shot, and it can't lean heavily on VFX.
- **0:51–7:52 countdown, 10 to 1** (about 40 s each): M 1:00, Sunrise 1:44, El Topo 2:25, Rashomon 2:49, Stalker 3:24, Night of the Hunter 4:01, Barry Lyndon 4:42, Blade Runner 5:33, Seven Samurai 6:31, Citizen Kane 7:19.
- **8:12 CTA:** an ironic "like / subscribe / bell" outro.

## Edit format (reproducible)
1. Run one continuous dry, witty VO. Picture is wall-to-wall film clips, with **no talking head**.
2. Each entry opens on a **lower-third card**: a gold rank-number box, the title in white, the DP's name in small type underneath, and a red "wolfcrow" tag on a translucent dark bar. Set it bottom-left, over the film's most iconic frame.
3. Spend 4–8 clips per entry. Each clip is held long enough to read its light.
4. Use ironic subtitle snippets from the films as gags
5. Build the escalation into the script: "What can beat Blade Runner and Barry Lyndon? Only two movies." It's a delayed-gratification countdown.

**Pacing:** ffmpeg scene detection finds 77 cuts at 0.3 (ASL 6.7 s) and 214 at 0.12 (ASL 2.4 s). True ASL is about 3–4 s; dark B&W hides cuts.

## Cinematography rules it teaches (apply to ad shots)
- **Production design carries the frame.** Most entries win because of real, detailed sets (Sunrise, Blade Runner). Brief art direction first.
- **Light hard and expressionistically for menace or mystery.** Hard shadows, silhouettes as a recurring visual signature (Night of the Hunter), and deep pools of black (M).
- **Shoot natural light, bounced and controlled.** Rashomon used mirrors to push sun through foliage. Barry Lyndon's lesson is to "nail the basics and you don't need fancy grading": painterly, soft, single-source window light and candlelight at very fast apertures.
- **Two-tone grade separates worlds.** Stalker is sepia in reality and colour in the Zone.
- **Mixed coloured practicals plus haze plus grain** give the Blade Runner look: cyan/amber neons, smoke and backlight.
- **Real weather on location** (rain, wind, multi-camera action in Seven Samurai) reads as truth.
- **Deep focus with long, complicated takes** (Citizen Kane) stages several planes of action in one frame instead of cutting.

## Tools
Film excerpts are fair use, so licence or regenerate the look instead for ads. The lower-third card is free in Remotion/HyperFrames. The VO is self-recorded.

## Mistakes it warns about
- Picking films for one great shot instead of sustained quality.
- Relying on VFX or matte paintings over real sets (Metropolis versus Sunrise).
- Being "enamored with f/1": shallow focus as a crutch.
- Assuming grading fixes bad lighting.
- Believing digital equals film for multi-coloured light. Blade Runner's skin tones, grain and highlight roll-off are "unique to film". When emulating it, add grain and highlight roll-off.

## Transferable to cinematic-ad
1. Open with a pattern-interrupt line plus self-aware humour before the "rules".
2. Keep a ranked countdown with a verbal "what can beat this?" bridge to hold attention across blocks.
3. Use one lower-third system (number, title, credit) as the consistency glue across blocks.
4. Choose a single lighting signature per block (silhouette, candle, neon haze) instead of mixing.
5. Separate the problem and solution worlds with two grade palettes.

- Beat 3: she uploads three files before the chatbot can answer.
- Beats 4–5: the chatbot gives instructions, while the folder still says “0 of 3 files changed.”
- Beats 7–8: she manually updates Excel and repairs malformed Word formatting.
- Beats 10–13: the downloaded deck loses its template, turns the chart into bullets, and creates version sprawl.
- Beat 13a, if included: the chatbot asks her to upload everything again.

Is it unmistakable? Yes in the main cut. The causal chain is unusually clear: chat answer → unchanged files → manual work. One slight ambiguity remains: the final wrong slide could look like Maya’s own mistake rather than the chatbot’s broken output, though the preceding comparison makes the intended meaning recoverable.

3. PAIN: 8/10.

Hardest-hitting beats:

- Beat 5: “0 of 3 files changed.” This is the thesis made visible.
- Beat 8: the pasted Calibri/`**€74.6m**` seam inside the real memo.
- Beat 15: the wrong number has already reached the partners.

Weak, generic or too long:

- Beat 1’s late-night setup is familiar finance-ad language.
- Beat 9’s “Partners want a pre-read” is functional but generic.
- Beat 17’s “The professional is the operating system” feels like a slogan explaining an emotion already understood.

4. FACE: Maya’s arc lands: tired setup → recognition → disbelief → shock/stillness → controlled reversal → relief. It escalates emotionally, and N6 is the strongest face beat.

N2 Recognition and N3 Disbelief are somewhat redundant: both are restrained reactions to screen failure. N6 and the laptop-closing beat also overlap as “silent devastation.” The missing beat is a clearly visible moment of choice when she reviews and approves a specific change; N8 gestures toward it, but an actual hand movement to Accept would make the agency more human and satisfying.

5. BUY: 7/10.

The proof is credible: same folder, same three files, inline changes, tracked Word change, deck change, per-file Changes panel and “10 of 10 accepted.” The strongest product claim is demonstrated rather than narrated.

What is missing is proof that the three files remain coherent after approval—ideally a final quick glimpse of the matching number across model, memo and deck. “Accept all” also weakens the review promise: a skeptical buyer may wonder whether she actually inspected the changes.

Clichés: black end card, late-night office fatigue, red-circled mistake, and “The professional is the operating system.” Distrust could arise from the perfect “10 of 10” result, the absence of a rejected change, and the disclaimer admitting mistakes without showing how rejection works.

6. CLARITY & PACING: The central message is readable, but the first half contains a lot of small operational detail: three uploads, a prompt, a numbered answer, folder status, Excel, Word, a text message, a download, a comparison and version sprawl.

I would worry about phone scroll-away around 0:28, when the film moves from the clear “0 of 3 files changed” thesis into a longer manual grind. The upload sequence may also feel slow before the viewer understands the payoff. The 75-second cut is sharper, but dropping N8 removes one of the few human moments showing control returning.
